CWM — ServiceNow's work-management product, its answer to Asana or Monday.com — runs on the same platform as an enterprise's IT incidents, HR cases and project approvals, and integrates with Project Workspace and Enterprise Agile Planning. No standalone tool can see that much. Six months ago CWM shipped the measurement layer it was missing: burndown, scope change, progress by status. Measurement tells a delivery lead the sprint is slipping. It can't tell her which three items did it, or what to change before standup in twenty minutes. This case study is about the next layer: judgment, and the ability to act on it without configuring anything first.

My perspective: 2.5 years as a software engineer on CWM across four SPM teams — sprint metrics, data grids, agile components — plus 50+ customer case tasks in the PPM and Project Workspace domain. I watched the product grow, and I watched people not start their day in it. This is my proposal for what would change that.

Act 01 — What CWM is, and where it stands

Collaboration inside the enterprise. That was the aim.

CWM stands for Collaborative Work Management. It's ServiceNow's product for everyday work inside the Now Platform — the same platform that runs ITSM, HR, customer service and portfolio management for 85% of the Fortune 500. The aim was a simpler, friendlier layer on top of the enterprise: boards, docs and tasks together, so nobody has to leave for Asana or Monday.com.

The team has shipped steadily since January 2024, and the collaboration surface is clean and genuinely usable. Build quality was never the problem. So here's the question this case study answers: what turns a product people tried into the one they open first?

Real momentum, from a small base. Against thousands of enterprise customers, almost all with dedicated SPM teams, this is a product being discovered rather than depended on: tried by some teams, and the place none of them start their day.

The pieces are already there

Some teams run agile sprints in it. Others use Connected Work to pull records from across the platform onto a board. It integrates with Project Workspace for planned work and with EAP for sprint execution. Individually, each is adopted. Collectively, they aren't yet connected into anything that earns the first click of the day — and that gap, not a missing feature, is the subject of this case study.

Act 02 — What shipped

The measurement layer is in.

In the last six months CWM shipped a work dashboard: burndown against the ideal line, a scope-change view, progress by status. For this case study it's the most important thing that has happened to the product — not because it settles the argument, but because it moves the starting line.

The thesis, in one line

The dashboard measures. A delivery lead has to judge. Everything that follows is about the layer between those two words.

Why shipping it was the right call

The team didn't ship another board template. It shipped a surface whose only job is telling you where things stand — a bet that CWM's value is in what it tells you, not what it stores. This proposal makes the same bet, one layer up.

And it built the expensive part

A burndown line needs a daily snapshot of sprint state. Once work is captured as time series, prediction becomes buildable — overload, cycle-time deviation, stalled items. Trend data was the hard prerequisite. It exists now, so none of the judgment layer starts from zero.

Market and competitor evidence

The escalation finding is the one that shaped this most. When configuration divergence is the single most common root cause of customer pain, the problem isn't that the product can't be shaped to the customer. It's that value is contingent on someone shaping it — which is the argument in Act 04. It came from support data, not from a whiteboard.

The adoption drop is the counterweight. It's the reason this proposal treats trust and accuracy as kill criteria rather than as polish, and why two of the four success metrics are counter-metrics.

The gap — and the two weeks that would close it

I have not run structured interviews with delivery leads. Everything here is grounded in support data, product knowledge and secondary research, which is enough to form a thesis and not enough to bet a roadmap on. Before writing a line of code I'd spend two weeks and no engineering time on this:

Who: 8–10 scrum masters and delivery leads across three customers already running sprints in CWM, plus three teams that left for an external tool.

What I'd ask: What did you open first this morning, and why? What did you do in the ten minutes after seeing the burndown off track? Where do your untracked follow-ups live right now? When did you last configure something in CWM, and who actually did it? What would make you stop paying for the other tool? Then three on the workspace itself: predict your most-used view before I show you the data; which of the two dashboards do you think of as yours; and how do you actually decide who gets which story?

What would falsify this: if leads tell me the dashboard is sufficient, or that they'd rather act on the sprint inside EAP than inside CWM, then the judgment layer is the wrong bet and the money belongs in the Project Workspace and EAP integrations instead. I'd want to know that in week two, not in quarter three.

Act 04 — The gaps

Two gaps between a measured sprint and a decided one.

Measurement is solved. These two are not. The first is why the product can't answer the morning question. The second is why it is hard to get value from at all.

A morning in the life

Priya, a scrum master, has standup in twenty minutes. She opens the dashboard first. The burndown is above the ideal line, so she knows the sprint is slipping. She does not know which three items caused it, that one is blocked by an unassigned incident, or that a dependency cleared at 11pm. So she opens EAP, then ITSM, then PPM, then her email, and joins it up by hand. Fifteen minutes later she has her answer, and acts on it in three more places. The chart told her something was wrong. It could not tell her what, or why, or let her do anything about it.

Gap 01

The chart says what. It never says why.

The dashboard shows the line is off. It cannot name the three items that put it there.
The reason is almost always in another module — an unassigned incident, a pending approval, a plan change.
So the user opens four more places and builds the causal picture by hand, every morning.

The fix

FR-1 names the items behind the line and why each one moved. FR-6 reaches across modules for the cause and shows the impact chain. Phase 1 and Phase 2.

Gap 02 · the bigger one

The product asks the user to design it first.

Configuration in CWM isn't locked behind an admin. It's handed to the user, in a side panel.
Before the board is useful, that user picks the view. Then the columns. Then the lanes for the kanban. Then the grouping, and the fields.
Not one of those is a decision a person has any basis to make on their first day.
And it isn't decided once. The same board needs a different shape at every stage of the work, so the panel gets reopened every time that changes.
In the escalations I triaged, the gap between configured behaviour and expected behaviour was the most common root cause of customer pain.
The category knows this failure: in ClickUp, teams create views they never open and then can't find the ones they need, and new users get lost working out which view is relevant to their job.

A settings panel isn't a feature. It's a question the product couldn't answer.

The fix — in two steps

Phase 1 — the panel proposes instead of asking. Under FR-12 a new board arrives with its view, columns and lanes already set for the work about to be done. The panel stays exactly where it is, for anyone who wants to change something — it just stops being the price of entry. Rule-based on work type and sprint state, so it needs no usage history to work on day one.

Phase 2 — stop asking. FR-9 learns from what the team actually does and moves the surface with the work: kanban while a sprint runs, list at assignment time, card view during grooming, timeline when a plan is in play. Suggested, never locked. Set out in full in Act 07.

The SMB test — a constraint, not a market

If a team of six can't get something useful out of a board in ten minutes without opening the settings panel, it isn't finished.

That holds for enterprises too, because the unit that adopts software is never the enterprise. It is the team of eight inside the 40,000-person customer: no budget line, no mandate, and no patience for a settings panel. That team is already paying for an external tool on someone's corporate card, and the live displacement deals show it can be won back.

Act 05 — Why CWM

A notification reaches one person.
A workspace lets a team decide.

The hardest objection to this proposal isn't from Asana. It's internal: if reasoning across ITSM, HR, PPM and EAP is valuable, it belongs to the platform — a Now Assist surface every user gets — not to a work-management app with a few thousand monthly users. It is half right, and the half that holds is worth conceding before anything else.

Conceded

The intelligence should be built as platform capability — Now Assist skills, reusable by any consumer, owned by the platform AI team. I'm not proposing CWM build a private AI stack. The question isn't who owns the reasoning. It's where a person receives it, and where they act on it.

A notification is one-way, private and disposable. It reaches an individual and dies in an inbox. But judgment about shared work has to be discussed before it becomes a decision, and a notification layer has nothing shared to attach that discussion to.

Three reasons that surface is CWM

01 · A notification tells. A workspace decides.

A platform briefing can say a sprint is at risk, then deep-link one person into five apps to act — the fragmentation it was meant to solve. In CWM the same insight is an object on a shared surface: the team sees it, comments on it, assigns from it, and the reasoning becomes a thread rather than a message in one inbox. The incumbents are a long way from this: in Jira, board view settings apply per user, so even the picture of the work is a private preference.

02 · CWM already holds the joins

Integrations with Project Workspace (planned work) and EAP (sprint execution), plus Connected Work for records from any table. The insight comes from joining planned, committed and ad-hoc work, and CWM is the one place those three already meet. A new platform surface would have to rebuild every one of those connections.

03 · A quality bar you can actually hit

Platform-wide means every persona and every module on day one, and reasoning accuracy is the thing that kills this product. One audience, one set of joins, one accuracy target — then expand once the predictions hold.

The platform should own the reasoning. CWM should own the morning.

And externally, the advantage is structural

A board in Asana or Monday is blind. It knows only what you typed into it. A board in CWM can reach the sprint, the incident, the approval and the project plan because they share a database, an identity model and a permission system. That isn't an integration anyone can buy — and the value isn't seeing the incident on a board. It's knowing it blocks your sprint story and the sprint ends Thursday.

EAP + ITSM + sprint deadline
"API migration at risk — blocking incident unresolved, sprint ends in 2 days."
Project Workspace + sprint scope
"The plan moved this milestone in. Your sprint is now 3 stories short of it."
Board activity + cycle time
"This item is stuck 3× longer than your team's average. Split or reassign?"

Each one crosses two or three systems. None of them is a chart, and none of them is buildable outside this platform.

Act 07 — The workspace

Judgment about what to show, not only what to say.

The Pulse answers what matters. A second question sits right beside it, and no work tool answers it: which of this workspace's surfaces should I be looking at right now? Today it's a manual choice, remade several times a day, by people with no visibility into what they actually use.

The views multiply, and the usage is invisible.

List, kanban, card, timeline. Each is right for a different moment — list when work is being assigned, kanban while a sprint runs, card view during grooming, timeline when a plan is being sequenced — and each stays on screen long after that moment has passed. This is a documented failure mode, not a hunch. In ClickUp, every list gets board, calendar, Gantt and timeline views by default, so teams create views they never open and then can't find the ones they need; the recommended fix is to pick two or three defaults and hide the rest. The sharper complaint is that flexibility becomes "which view do I use for what?" without a team playbook, and a new user in a large workspace gets lost working out which view is relevant to their job — the single biggest source of friction in the first weeks.

And nobody is telling the customer. Atlassian's admin analytics report managed accounts, done rates and AI active users. Asana's admin console shows which teams and individuals use it most, plus task activity over time. Seats and activity — not which views a team opens, or how that changed across the last six sprints. ServiceNow has platform usage telemetry that could answer exactly that, for the customer and for us.

And two dashboards are two destinations.

A personal work dashboard and a board-level dashboard, sitting as siblings, make the user decide which one is theirs on every visit. The failure mode is familiar from every tool that has tried it: information density is not the same as usefulness, and a wall of data with no hierarchy is just noise. One of those two should stop being a destination. My Work holds the Pulse — personal, cross-project, judgment. Board measurement belongs inside the board, as the answer to "how is this team tracking," not as a rival place to go.

Step one: suggest the setup, don't demand it.

The cheapest win needs no usage history at all. Today the side panel asks three questions: which view, which columns, which lanes. It should arrive with them answered — a sprint board with sprint columns, a grooming session in card view — and stay available for anyone who wants to change something. Rules, not learning. This is FR-12, and it ships in Phase 1 because inverting a panel from asking to proposing is the fastest way to make the first ten minutes useful.

Step two: stop asking. The view follows the work.

Once there is usage history, the surface can move with the moment instead of being selected. The platform already knows the phase — whether a sprint is running, being assigned, being groomed, or whether a plan is in play — and the telemetry says which view that team actually reaches for at each of those points.

The Pulse
A default, never a lock

The surface is suggested, one click changes it, and the change is remembered. Anything stronger is a magic trick that fights the user. The same telemetry pays for a second output almost free: show an admin which views their teams actually open, by phase, over time — the mirror no vendor in this category offers.

And at assignment time, the judgment nobody has.

The assignment view is where the hardest questions of a sprint get answered by instinct. Who should take this? Has the split been fair? Is anyone drawing the same work every sprint? Does the order respect what blocks what? A work tool that treats a person as a bucket with capacity cannot help with any of it.

Distribution signals — described, never decided

"The regression work has gone to the same person three sprints running. Two others have taken none." "These two stories should go first — four other items wait on them."

This is the strongest example of the moat in the whole proposal, because answering it needs work history, item type, sprint sequence and the people data on the HR side of the platform. Asana can't reach that. Neither can EAP alone.

The line I would not cross: describe the distribution of work, never assess the person. Team-aggregate by default, opt-in at team level, surfaced only to the person already accountable for the allocation. No scores on people, no rankings, no recommended names — a model naming who gets which story, inside a platform that also runs HR, is a governance project rather than a feature. On the state of the art: Asana already suggests assignees from workload and skill tags, which answers who can do the work. Fairness, repetition and range are a different question, and nobody is answering it.

Why this is the same thesis, not a new one

Fewer and better defaults is a bet that has already won this market once. Linear's thesis was that teams don't need custom workflows, they need defaults matching how teams actually work — and a fifty-engineer team onboards in an afternoon with zero configuration, where the same team on Jira needs two to four weeks of admin setup. Narrowing choice is the product. That's the same argument as Act 04, applied to the interface instead of the insight.

Act 10 — The hard calls

Four objections this would face in its first review.

01
"The dashboard just shipped. Isn't this the same thing with AI on top?"
The tensionBurndown, scope change and progress by status already tell a team how work is tracking. A briefing layer arriving months later can read as a wrapper around a feature the team just built — or worse, a second surface answering the same question.
Where I landThe dashboard is the substrate, not the competition. It answers "how are we doing," and nobody's morning ends there. What follows is why the line moved, what's blocking those items outside this board, and what to change before standup — which needs cause and effect across modules a chart can't reach. It also built the daily snapshots every prediction depends on. The hardest prerequisite is already done, and the Pulse embeds the dashboard rather than competing with it.
02
"Zero configuration isn't possible here. Permissions and licensing decide what a user can see."
The tensionACLs and role-based access are why the platform is configuration-heavy. A Pulse that surfaces an ITSM incident to a scrum master with no ITSM role either leaks data or shows an empty state. Entitlements compound it — seeing records from a module can require a licence for that module. "Make it the default" doesn't survive contact with either.
Where I landTwo tiers, and the summary tier is the product. A Pulse item states the fact and the impact — "blocked by an unresolved incident, sprint ends Thursday" — derived server-side and requiring no source-table permission, because it exposes no record data. The record itself, and the deep link to it, appear only for users whose existing role and entitlement already allow it; everyone else sees the impact and who to ask. That keeps first value free of setup without inventing an access path the platform doesn't sanction. It's the requirement I'd validate with a platform architect before anything else.
03
"A tool that picks the view for you takes control away from the user."
The tensionUsers have preferences, and a workspace that rearranges itself is disorienting — worse than a cluttered one they've learned. There's a real risk of building a clever thing people fight every morning, and view intent is guessing at intent from state, which will sometimes be wrong.
Where I landSuggest, never lock, and remember the override. The alternative isn't neutrality — it's a default chosen by whoever configured the workspace last, which is no less of an imposition and usually a worse guess. Getting this wrong is cheap and reversible, unlike the reasoning requirements: a wrong view costs one click, a wrong "at risk" costs trust. It also has a built-in honesty check, because the same telemetry that drives the suggestion measures the override rate. If people override more than a third of the time, the phase model is wrong and should be pulled.
04
"ServiceNow is enterprise software. A team of six isn't our customer."
The tensionSeat economics, partner-led implementation and a configuration-first heritage all point away from lightweight self-serve. Designing to a ten-minute bar looks like designing for a segment we don't sell to, and invites the question of whether this is a mid-market strategy nobody asked for.
Where I landSMB here is a constraint, not a market. The unit that adopts software is never the enterprise — it's the team of eight inside the 40,000-seat customer, with no mandate and no patience, already paying for an external tool on a corporate card. Designing for that team is how we win back accounts being lost inside our own installed base, and the live displacement deals show it's winnable. If it later makes a genuine mid-market motion possible, I'd take the upside. I'm not claiming it here.

Any tool can give you boards and docs.

Only CWM can make them aware

of your entire enterprise.

The measurement layer is built. Judgment is the layer no standalone tool can copy and no other surface is positioned to deliver — because CWM sits on the same database as your incidents and approvals, and already joins your plans to your sprints. That's not an integration. That's a moat.
