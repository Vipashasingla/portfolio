Connecting dots
no other tool
can see.

CWM — ServiceNow's work-management product, its answer to Asana or Monday.com — runs on the same platform as an enterprise's IT incidents, HR cases and project approvals, and integrates with Project Workspace and Enterprise Agile Planning. No standalone tool can reach that much. Six months ago CWM shipped the measurement layer it was missing: burndown, scope change, progress by status. Measurement tells a delivery lead the sprint is slipping. It can't tell her which three items did it, or what to change before standup in twenty minutes. This case study is about that next layer — judgment, and the ability to act on it, without asking anyone to configure something first.

My perspective: 2.5 years as a software engineer on CWM across four SPM teams — sprint metrics, data grids, agile components — plus 50+ customer case tasks in the PPM and Project Workspace domain. I watched the product grow, and I watched people not start their day in it. This is my proposal for what would change that.

Act 01 — What CWM is, and where it stands

Collaboration inside the enterprise. That was the aim.

CWM stands for Collaborative Work Management. It's ServiceNow's product for everyday work inside the Now Platform — the same platform that runs ITSM, HR, customer service and portfolio management for 85% of the Fortune 500. The aim was a simpler, friendlier layer on top of the enterprise: boards, docs and tasks together, so nobody has to leave for Asana or Monday.com.

It has shipped at pace since January 2024 and the collaboration surface is clean and genuinely usable. Build quality was never the problem. So here's the question this case study answers: what turns a product people tried into the one they open first?

Real momentum, from a small base. Set against a customer base of thousands of enterprises, almost all with dedicated SPM teams, the picture is a product being discovered rather than depended on — tried by some teams, not the place SPM users start their day.

The pieces are already there

Teams run agile sprints in it. Others pull records from across the platform onto boards through Connected Work. It integrates with Project Workspace for planned project work and with EAP for sprint execution. Individually, each is adopted. Collectively, they aren't yet connected into anything that earns the first click of the day — and that gap, not a missing feature, is the subject of this case study.

Act 02 — What shipped

The measurement layer is in.

In the last six months CWM shipped a work dashboard: burndown against the ideal line, a scope-change view, progress by status. For this case study it's the most important thing that has happened to the product — not because it settles the argument, but because it moves the starting line.

The thesis, in one line

The dashboard measures. A delivery lead has to judge. This case study is the layer between those two words.

Why shipping it was the right call

The team didn't ship another board template. It shipped a surface whose only job is telling you where things stand — a bet that CWM's value is in what it tells you, not what it stores. This proposal makes the same bet, one layer up.

And it built the expensive part

A burndown line needs a daily snapshot of sprint state. Once work is captured as time series, prediction becomes buildable — overload, cycle-time deviation, stalled items. Trend data was the hard prerequisite. It exists now, so none of the judgment layer starts from zero.

How I formed this view — and what would change it.

Four sources, and one gap I'd rather name than paper over.

Five core UI surfaces across four SPM teams, including sprint metrics and the agile components.
50+ customer case tasks
PPM and Project Workspace domain — real customer problems, in their own words, in writing.
Escalation root-cause triage
Around a quarter traced to product behaviour diverging from user expectation, not misconfiguration.
Adoption data, adjacent AI feature
A sharp drop after launch in another SPM product. Shipping an AI surface and having it used are different problems.

The escalation finding is the one that shaped this most. When configuration divergence is the single most common root cause of customer pain, the problem isn't that the product can't be shaped to the customer. It's that value is contingent on someone shaping it — which is the argument in Act 04, arrived at from support data rather than from a whiteboard.

The adoption drop is the counterweight. It's the reason this proposal treats trust and accuracy as kill criteria rather than as polish, and why two of the four success metrics are counter-metrics.

The gap — and the two weeks that would close it

I have not run structured interviews with delivery leads. Everything here is grounded in support data, product knowledge and secondary research, which is enough to form a thesis and not enough to bet a roadmap on. Before writing a line of code I'd spend two weeks and no engineering time on this:

Who: 8–10 scrum masters and delivery leads across three customers already running sprints in CWM, plus three teams that left for an external tool.

What I'd ask: What did you open first this morning, and why? What did you do in the ten minutes after seeing the burndown off track? Where do your untracked follow-ups live right now? When did you last configure something in CWM, and who actually did it? What would make you stop paying for the other tool?

What would falsify this: if leads tell me the dashboard is sufficient, or that they'd rather act on the sprint inside EAP than inside CWM, then the judgment layer is the wrong bet and the money belongs in the Project Workspace and EAP integrations instead. I'd want to know that in week two, not in quarter three.

Act 04 — The gap

From a chart to a decision.

Measurement is solved. Three gaps sit between a measured sprint and a decided one, and each is a reason the product doesn't get opened first.

A morning in the life

Priya, a scrum master on a delivery team, has standup in twenty minutes. She opens the dashboard first now — burndown above the ideal line, progress flat — so she knows the sprint is slipping. She doesn't know which three items caused it, that one is waiting on an unassigned incident, or that a dependency cleared at 11pm. So she opens EAP, then the ITSM module, then PPM, and assembles the rest by hand. Fifteen minutes later she writes three follow-ups on a sticky note, because they're too small for a card. The chart told her something was wrong. It couldn't tell her what to do about it, and it had nowhere to put what she decided.

Gap 1 — Measurement, but no diagnosis.

The dashboard says the line moved. The reason it moved is always in a different module, so the user assembles the causal picture by hand, every morning:

CWM dashboard
"Is the sprint on track?" — tells her it isn't. Not which items, not why.
EAP
"Which stories moved?" — no blocker visible from here.
ITSM module
"What's blocking them?" — the incident, unassigned, with no sprint context.
PPM / Project Workspace
"What's waiting on approval?" — no link to task-level impact.
Email / Slack
"What landed overnight?" — unranked, unconnected to any work item.

Context-switching cost is well documented — Harvard Business Review (2022); Cornell University & Qatalog. This is that tax, paid inside a single platform.

Gap 2 — Nowhere to put small work.

Chase the vendor invoice. Follow up with Priya after standup. Read the doc before Thursday. Roughly half of what fills a working day never reaches a project, and CWM wants a board, a card and a state before it will accept an item — so that half goes to sticky notes, self-DMs and flagged email instead. Asana and Monday won this ground by making capture nearly free: a keystroke, no fields. The plainest reason a work tool doesn't get opened first is that the things filling your day aren't in it.

It cuts the other way too. A briefing you can only read is a newsletter. If the Pulse tells Priya a story is blocked and her only option is to go elsewhere to act, she'll go elsewhere and stay there. Read, decide, capture, close — the dashboard delivers the first step and nothing after it.

Gap 3 — Nothing works until someone configures it.

ServiceNow's greatest strength is that it can be shaped to any enterprise on earth. The cost is that most surfaces are worth something only once somebody shapes them: fields, states, roles, templates, a defined sprint. Each one is a payment the user makes before the product pays anything back — and in the escalations I triaged, that gap between configured behaviour and expected behaviour was the most common root cause of customer pain.

The dashboard is a fair illustration. It's genuinely useful, and useful to teams already running sprints in CWM — a modest fraction of the customer base. That's the ceiling on anything gated behind setup: real value, delivered to the people who already paid.

The SMB test — a constraint, not a market

If a team of six people with no administrator can't get something useful out of it in ten minutes, it isn't finished.

I'd apply that to every requirement, and it holds for enterprises, because the unit that actually adopts software is never the enterprise. It's the team of eight inside the 40,000-person customer: no admin, no budget line, no patience. That team is the one paying for an external tool on someone's corporate card today, and the live displacement deals are evidence it can be won back. Whether this eventually makes a genuine mid-market motion possible is upside I'd take — not the bet I'm making here.

Act 05 — Why CWM

The platform should own the reasoning. CWM should own the morning.

The hardest objection to this proposal isn't from Asana. It's internal: if reasoning across ITSM, HR, PPM and EAP is valuable, it belongs to the platform — a Now Assist surface every user gets — not to a work-management app with a few thousand monthly users. That objection is half right, so it's worth conceding the half that is.

Conceded

The intelligence should be built as platform capability — Now Assist skills, reusable by any consumer, owned by the platform AI team. I'm not proposing CWM build a private AI stack. The question isn't who owns the reasoning. It's where a person receives it, and where they act on it.

Four reasons that surface is CWM

01 · A briefing needs somewhere to land

A platform-level briefing can tell you a sprint is at risk, then deep-link you into five different apps to do something about it — which is the fragmentation it was meant to solve. CWM is the only SPM surface that already holds boards, docs and tasks, so orientation and execution sit in one place.

02 · CWM already holds the joins

Integrations with Project Workspace (planned work) and EAP (sprint execution), plus Connected Work for records from any table. The insight comes from the join between planned, committed and ad-hoc work — and CWM is where those three edges already meet. A new platform surface would have to rebuild them.

03 · Nothing to displace

ITSM and HR users have entrenched workspaces and morning habits; changing their front door is a fight with an incumbent. CWM's low usage is the asset here — there's no first-open habit to break, which makes it the cheapest place in the portfolio to establish a new one.

04 · A quality bar you can actually hit

Platform-wide means every persona and every module on day one, and reasoning accuracy is the thing that kills this product. One audience, one set of joins, one accuracy target — then expand once the predictions hold.

And externally, the advantage is structural

A board in Asana or Monday is blind. It knows only what you typed into it. A board in CWM can reach the sprint, the incident, the approval and the project plan because they share a database, an identity model and a permission system. That isn't an integration anyone can buy — and the value isn't seeing the incident on a board. It's knowing it blocks your sprint story and the sprint ends Thursday.

EAP + ITSM + sprint deadline
"API migration at risk — blocking incident unresolved, sprint ends in 2 days."
Project Workspace + sprint scope
"The plan moved this milestone in. Your sprint is now 3 stories short of it."
Board activity + cycle time
"This item is stuck 3× longer than your team's average. Split or reassign?"

Each one crosses two or three systems. None of them is a chart, and none of them is buildable outside this platform.

Daily Pulse.

Three answers the dashboard can't give, then one thing it can't do. Each card below is what a user would actually read on screen.

⚡

Why the line moved

Not "the sprint is behind." The three items behind it, and the reason each one moved — added scope, stalled, or blocked by something outside this board.

△

Not "15 items moved state." Instead: "the blocking incident cleared at 11pm — pull that story into the sprint before standup." Cause and effect across systems.

◎

"Committed above your historical velocity. Dependency cascade from Team A to Team C." Predictions, from the time series the dashboard already collects.

Each item carries its actions inline. One click, no navigation, no form.

Quick capture — the smallest unit of work

One field. No project, no board, no state, no owner. Type "chase the vendor invoice" and it's on tomorrow's Pulse if it isn't done. Promote it to a board item later, only if it grows into something that deserves one.

It's the only part of the product that passes the ten-minute test on day one — which is why it ships in the first phase beside the intelligence rather than after it.

Act 09 — The hard calls

Three objections this would face in its first review.

01
"The dashboard just shipped. Isn't this the same thing with AI on top?"
The tensionBurndown, scope change and progress by status already tell a team how work is tracking. A briefing layer arriving months later can read as a wrapper around a feature the team just built — or worse, a second surface answering the same question.
Where I landThe dashboard is the substrate, not the competition. It answers "how are we doing," and nobody's morning ends there. What follows is why the line moved, what's blocking those items outside this board, and what to change before standup — which needs cause and effect across modules a chart can't reach. It also built the daily snapshots every prediction depends on. The hardest prerequisite is already done, and the Pulse embeds the dashboard rather than competing with it.
02
"Zero configuration isn't possible here. Permissions and licensing decide what a user can see."
The tensionACLs and role-based access are why the platform is configuration-heavy. A Pulse that surfaces an ITSM incident to a scrum master with no ITSM role either leaks data or shows an empty state. Entitlements compound it — seeing records from a module can require a licence for that module. "Make it the default" doesn't survive contact with either.
Where I landTwo tiers, and the summary tier is the product. A Pulse item states the fact and the impact — "blocked by an unresolved incident, sprint ends Thursday" — derived server-side and requiring no source-table permission, because it exposes no record data. The record itself, and the deep link to it, appear only for users whose existing role and entitlement already allow it; everyone else sees the impact and who to ask. That keeps first value free of setup without inventing an access path the platform doesn't sanction. It's the requirement I'd validate with a platform architect before anything else.
03
"ServiceNow is enterprise software. A team of six isn't our customer."
The tensionSeat economics, partner-led implementation and a configuration-first heritage all point away from lightweight self-serve. Designing to a ten-minute bar looks like designing for a segment we don't sell to, and invites the question of whether this is a mid-market strategy nobody asked for.
Where I landSMB here is a constraint, not a market. The unit that adopts software is never the enterprise — it's the team of eight inside the 40,000-seat customer, with no admin and no patience, already paying for an external tool on a corporate card. Designing for that team is how we win back accounts being lost inside our own installed base, and the live displacement deals show it's winnable. If it later makes a genuine mid-market motion possible, I'd take the upside. I'm not claiming it here.
